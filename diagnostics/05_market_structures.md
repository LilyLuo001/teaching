## Why this quiz exists

Q13) and again, identically, in February/March 2020 (9708/32/F/M/20, Q11). Both times, more students chose the *monopoly* answer than the correct one
(46% vs 25%, then 39% vs 37%). The four options on that question are four separate
misconceptions, and this quiz is built around them:

| 考点 | Misconception | Taught in |
|---|---|---|
| **1** | Supernormal profit survives into the long run | video F1 |
| **2** | The long-run point sits at minimum average cost | video F1 |
| **3** | The demand curve is more elastic than perfect competition | **quiz only (so far)** |
| **4** | There are more firms than under perfect competition | **quiz only (so far)** |

考点 3 and 4 are the hook at the end of F1 — the video says it ran out of time and
sends the viewer here. So items 5 and 6 must be strong; they are the payoff for the

### Q1 — 考点 1
A firm in a **monopolistically competitive** market is earning supernormal profit this
year. Barriers to entry are low. What is the most likely position in the long run?

- **A. Price equals average cost, and the firm earns normal profit** ✅
- B. Price remains above average cost, because the firm's product is differentiated
- C. Price falls below average cost and the firm exits
- D. Price equals marginal cost, and the firm is allocatively efficient

**Correct: A.** New firms enter, take a share of demand, and shift the individual
firm's demand curve left until it is tangent to average cost. AR = AC is normal profit.

- *B* — **考点 1, the core error.** Differentiation gives a downward-sloping demand
  curve; it does not block entry. This is the assumption behind option B on
  **CAIE 9708/32/O/N/16 Q13**, chosen by 46% of candidates against 25% correct.
- *C* — over-correction. Entry stops *at* normal profit, not past it; losses would
  cause exit and push the curve back.
- *D* — P = MC requires a horizontal demand curve. Here demand slopes down, so price
  stays above marginal cost even in the long run. See Q7.

### Q2 — 考点 1
Which single feature best explains why a **monopoly** can keep supernormal profit in
the long run but a monopolistically competitive firm cannot?

- **A. High barriers to entry** ✅
- B. The monopolist faces a downward-sloping demand curve
- C. The monopolist is the sole seller in the market
- D. The monopolist produces where marginal revenue equals marginal cost

**Correct: A.** Entry is the mechanism. Everything else follows from it.

- *B* — **both** structures have downward-sloping demand. This is precisely why the
  short-run diagrams look alike, and why it cannot be the distinguishing feature.
- *C* — true of a monopoly, but it is a *description* of the outcome, not the cause.
  Being the sole seller is only sustainable *because* entry is blocked. **CAIE 9708/43/O/N/23**, Q3 (report p.28) notes that identifying the key characteristics of each
  structure, even correctly, only "would have ensured a lower Level 2 mark" — the marks
  come from the mechanism, not the list.
- *D* — every profit-maximising firm in every structure does this. It separates
  nothing.

### Q3 — 考点 2
In long-run equilibrium, a monopolistically competitive firm's average revenue curve is
**tangent** to its average cost curve. Where does that tangency occur?

- **A. On the downward-sloping part of average cost, to the left of its minimum** ✅
- B. Exactly at the minimum point of average cost
- C. On the upward-sloping part of average cost, to the right of its minimum
- D. Wherever average cost crosses marginal cost

**Correct: A.** A downward-sloping AR can only touch a U-shaped AC on the falling
section. That is a geometric necessity, not a modelling choice.

- *B* — **考点 2, the central error.** Minimum AC is the *perfect competition*
  outcome, where horizontal AR is tangent at the bottom. Importing it here erases
  excess capacity, which is the answer to **CAIE 9708/32/O/N/16 Q13** (25% correct).
- *C* — would require an upward-sloping AR.
- *D* — MC cuts AC at its minimum, so this is option B restated. Choosing it usually
  means the tangency condition has been replaced by a memorised intersection rule.

### Q4 — 考点 2
**Excess capacity** in monopolistic competition refers to:

- **A. The gap between the firm's actual long-run output and the output that would minimise its average cost** ✅
- B. Unsold stock the firm cannot shift at the current price
- C. Machinery left idle because demand is below the firm's physical capacity
- D. The difference between the firm's output and the industry's total output

**Correct: A.** It is a comparison of two output levels on one cost curve — a
theoretical gap, not a warehouse.

- *B, C* — **the everyday-English reading.** "Capacity" in ordinary usage means
  physical plant, so students describe idle machines. The term here is defined purely
  by the position of the tangency. **CAIE 9708/32/F/M/20 Q11** (37% correct) states it as "a
  difference between the ideal (optimum) output and the output actually attained".
- *D* — confuses the firm with the industry.

### Q5 — 考点 3  ← *the hook item from video F1*
Compare the demand curve facing an individual firm in **monopolistic competition** with
the one facing an individual firm in **perfect competition**. The monopolistically
competitive firm's demand curve is:

- **A. Less price-elastic, because its product is differentiated** ✅
- B. More price-elastic, because there are many competing firms
- C. Equally elastic, since both structures have many firms
- D. Perfectly inelastic, because it has some price-setting power

**Correct: A.** In perfect competition the firm's demand curve is **horizontal** —
perfectly elastic, the most elastic a curve can be. Nothing can be *more* elastic than
that. Differentiation means a monopolistically competitive firm can raise price and
keep some customers, so its curve slopes down and is therefore **less** elastic.

- *B* — **考点 3, the error.** Reasoning from "many firms → lots of competition → very
  elastic" and overshooting the horizontal benchmark. This is option D on **CAIE 9708/32/O/N/16 Q13**;
  the examiner report states plainly: "The demand curve for the firm in
  perfect competition is horizontal, but for monopolistic competition it is downward
  sloping so it is not more price-elastic."
- *C* — treats firm numbers as the only determinant and ignores differentiation.
- *D* — confuses *having some* price-setting power with having unlimited price-setting
  power. Perfectly inelastic would mean no customer ever leaves at any price.

### Q6 — 考点 4  ← *the hook item from video F1*
Which statement about the **number of firms** correctly distinguishes monopolistic
competition from perfect competition in the long run?

- **A. It does not distinguish them — both have a large number of firms** ✅
- B. Monopolistic competition has more firms, because product differentiation creates niches
- C. Monopolistic competition has fewer firms, because differentiation is a barrier to entry
- D. Monopolistic competition has exactly one firm per differentiated product, so the count is the number of products

**Correct: A.** Both models assume many firms and easy entry. Firm count is not the
dividing line — excess capacity is.

- *B* — **考点 4, the error.** This is option C on **CAIE 9708/32/O/N/16 Q13**. The
  examiner report states both structures have "a large number of firms", so the option
  cannot be what you'd "most likely find" as a difference.
- *C* — differentiation is not a barrier to entry. A new entrant simply differentiates
  too. Treating it as a barrier is the step that turns monopolistic competition into a
  monopoly in a student's head.
- *D* — a definitional slide: it makes every firm a monopolist of its own product,
  which is exactly the confusion the whole quiz is about.

### Q7 — efficiency
In long-run equilibrium, a monopolistically competitive firm is:

- **A. Neither productively nor allocatively efficient** ✅
- B. Productively efficient but not allocatively efficient
- C. Allocatively efficient but not productively efficient
- D. Both, because entry has competed away all supernormal profit

**Correct: A.** Not productively efficient, because output is left of minimum AC — that
is excess capacity (考点 2). Not allocatively efficient, because the downward-sloping
demand curve leaves price above marginal cost (考点 3).

- *D* — **the most revealing wrong answer.** It assumes zero supernormal profit implies
  efficiency. It does not: normal profit is about the *level* of profit, efficiency is
  about the *position* of output. **AQA 7136/1, Jun 2023, Q9** set exactly this as an essay and
  reported that students who confused the two structures had "very little, if anything"
  that could be rewarded.
- *B* — would require production at minimum AC.
- *C* — would require P = MC.

### Q8 — application
A town has around forty hand car washes. Each is independently owned, prices differ by
a small amount, and anyone with modest savings can open another one. Several owners
report they are "not really making money beyond wages". What market structure is this,
and what is the single strongest piece of evidence?

- **A. Monopolistic competition — low barriers to entry, and returns have settled at normal profit** ✅
- B. Monopoly — each car wash sets its own price, so each has price-setting power
- C. Oligopoly — a small number of firms with differentiated products
- D. Perfect competition — the identical answer, since the service is essentially the same everywhere

**Correct: A.** Many firms, slight differentiation, free entry, and long-run normal
profit. The "not making money beyond wages" detail is the giveaway: normal profit.

- *B* — **the headline confusion, in applied form.** Price-setting power exists in
  *every* structure except perfect competition; it does not make a firm a monopoly.
  **Edexcel 9EC0/03, Jun 2021, Q1(a)** records that students wrote "monopolistic"
  without "competition", and states monopoly and oligopoly were "not allowed" as answers here.
- *C* — forty independent firms is not a small number, and no firm's decisions depend
  on a rival's.
- *D* — defensible enough that Edexcel accepted it with matching explanation, but
  weaker: prices differ between washes, which perfect competition rules out. Choose it
  and you lose the differentiation mark.
