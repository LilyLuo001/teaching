# Diagnostic 3 — Public, merit and demerit goods

**Module:** 专题 3 · **Items:** 5 · **Time:** 6 minutes
**Targets:** 16 examiner statements co-mentioning merit and public goods across 10 CAIE
reports, 2016–2024, inside the corpus's #2 topic (market failure, 113 statements).

The confusion has a specific structure: students treat "public" and "merit" as points
on one scale of *how much the government should provide something*. They are answers to
two different questions, which is why neither can be an example of the other.

---

### Q1
Which pair of characteristics defines a **pure public good**?

- **A. Non-rival and non-excludable in consumption** ✅
- B. Provided by the government and free at the point of use
- C. Under-consumed relative to the socially optimal level
- D. Generating positive externalities in consumption

**Correct: A.** The definition is a property of the *good*, independent of who supplies
it.

- *B* — **the central error**: defining a public good by its provider. State schools are
  government-provided and free at the point of use, and are neither non-rival nor
  non-excludable.
- *C, D* — these describe a **merit** good. Choosing either is the exact merit/public
  substitution examiners report.

---

### Q2
Which is the strongest example of a **merit good**?

- **A. Vaccination** ✅
- B. National defence
- C. Food
- D. Street lighting

**Correct: A.** Under-consumed because private benefit is undervalued, and it generates
external benefits — but it is entirely rival and excludable.

- *B, D* — **public goods offered as merit goods**, the specific error recorded in CAIE
  Jun 2018.
- *C* — **the imprecise example.** CAIE (Jun 2024) singles out candidates who "simply
  offered e.g. 'food'". Food is an ordinary private good; nothing about it implies
  systematic undervaluation. Examiners note demerit-good examples are usually sharper,
  so the weakness is specific to the merit case.

---

### Q3
A question states: *"Sea walls protecting a coastal town are a public good."* It then
asks you to explain why the free market will under-provide them. What should the answer
do with the classification given in the stem?

- **A. Use it: explain non-excludability, the free-rider problem, and the resulting absence of a profitable market** ✅
- B. Ignore it and explain the positive externalities of flood protection instead
- C. First argue about whether sea walls really are a pure public good
- D. Explain that the government provides sea walls because they are essential

**Correct: A.** When a stem hands you a classification, the marks are for *using* it.

- *B* — **exactly what OCR reported** (H460, Summer 2023): weaker responses "largely
  ignored the direction in the stem" that flood defences are public goods "and wrote at
  length about perceived positive externalities."
- *C* — spends the answer challenging a premise the question has already settled.
- *D* — asserts necessity instead of explaining the market-failure mechanism; also the
  #2 mistake in the corpus.

---

### Q4
Why does the free-rider problem prevent a private market from forming?

- **A. Because consumption cannot be withheld from non-payers, so there is no way to charge, and no profit-seeking firm will supply** ✅
- B. Because the good is too expensive for any private firm to produce
- C. Because consumers do not realise how much they benefit from the good
- D. Because the government has decided to supply it instead

**Correct: A.** Non-excludability destroys the ability to charge, which destroys the
revenue model.

- *B* — cost is not the issue; excludability is.
- *C* — **that is information failure, which defines merit goods, not public goods.**
  Picking C is the clearest possible signal the two categories have merged.
- *D* — reverses cause and effect.

---

### Q5
A demerit good is best described as one that is:

- **A. Over-consumed, because consumers overvalue its private benefit or underestimate its private cost** ✅
- B. Any good that generates a negative externality in production
- C. Any good the government taxes
- D. A good that is rival and excludable

**Correct: A.** The defining feature is the *consumer's* misvaluation.

- *B* — **conflates demerit goods with negative externalities.** A factory emitting
  pollution creates a negative production externality without its output being a
  demerit good. The externality is a third-party effect; the demerit property is an
  information failure affecting the consumer.
- *C* — defines the category by the policy response, the mirror of the Q1 error.
- *D* — true of most goods, so it distinguishes nothing.

---

## The discrimination this module teaches

Two independent axes, which students collapse into one:

| Question asked | Property of | Answer |
|---|---|---|
| Can consumers be excluded? Is consumption rival? | the **good** | public ↔ private |
| Do consumers value it correctly? | the **consumer's information** | merit ↔ demerit ↔ neither |

A good has a position on **both** axes at once. Vaccination is private on the first and
merit on the second. That is why "is it a public good or a merit good?" is a malformed
question — and why students who have merged the axes cannot answer consistently.
