## The one takeaway

> The difference is not in the diagram. It is in what happens *after* the diagram
> — entry. And entry decides both the long-run profit and the excess capacity.

## Script (approx. 700 words, 4:30 at teaching pace)

### [0:00] Hook

Cambridge set this question in the October/November 2016 series — A Level Paper 3, the multiple-choice paper. Only **25%** of students got it
right — and **46%** picked the same wrong answer.

Then, four years later, they set *the exact same question again.* Same stem, same
four options, same order. This time 37% got it right.

And that same wrong answer *still* beat the correct one.

So this is not a hard question. It is a question where most students are confident
— and wrong. Every one of those students made the same mistake: they treated
monopolistic competition as if it were a kind of monopoly.

Let's fix that, and then let's do the question.

### [0:20] Definition — the two things that matter

Two market structures. Forget the diagrams for a second, because the diagrams are
the trap. Start with entry.

**Monopoly.** One seller. Barriers to entry are *high* — a patent, a licence,
costs too big for anyone else to carry. And here is the consequence: that firm
makes supernormal profit, other firms can see it, and they still cannot get in.
So **in the long run, the supernormal profit stays.**

**Monopolistic competition.** Many sellers, each with a slightly different
product — different brand, different location, different feel. That
differentiation gives each firm a downward-sloping demand curve, which is exactly
why it *looks* like a monopoly. But barriers to entry are *low.*

So the same supernormal profit shows up, other firms see it, and this time they
walk straight in. New entrants take a share of your demand. Your demand curve
shifts left. And it keeps shifting until the profit is gone.

Two consequences, and these are the two things students lose marks on.

One: **in the long run there is only normal profit.**

Two — and this is the one almost nobody sees coming: because your demand curve
still slopes downward, it comes to rest *touching* your average cost curve rather
than crossing it. A tangent. And that tangency point sits to the **left** of the
lowest point on average cost. You end up producing less than the output that
would minimise your average cost. **That gap is called excess capacity.**

Remember those two. Long-run normal profit. Excess capacity.

### [1:05] The diagrams, side by side

Here they are together. Left, monopoly. Right, monopolistic competition. Short
run.

Look at them. Downward-sloping demand, both. MR below AR, both. Profit-maximising
where MR equals MC, both. Supernormal profit shaded, both.

They are almost the same picture. *This is why the confusion survives.* If you
compare short-run diagrams, there is genuinely very little to separate them.

So stop comparing the pictures. Run time forward instead.

Monopoly — nothing happens. Firms want in, the barrier holds, the profit block
sits exactly where it was.

Monopolistic competition — watch the right panel. Entrants arrive. The demand
curve slides left. The profit block shrinks. Again. Again. And it stops right
here, where average revenue just kisses average cost.

Two different long runs, from the same short run. That is the whole topic.

### [1:40] 常错考点 1 — long-run profit

First one. Does supernormal profit survive?

Monopoly: yes. Monopolistic competition: no — it is competed away to normal
profit, and normal profit means price equals average cost.

Watch what that means on the diagram: at that tangency point, AR and AC are
touching, so price *equals* average cost. Not above it.

Now Cambridge asks it like this.

### [2:10] 原题 1

Cambridge, May/June 2022, A Level Paper 3 — paper code 9708/31, question 9. You're shown a monopolistically
competitive firm in short-run equilibrium and asked what will **not** be true in
the long run.

One of the options is: *the profit-maximising price is greater than average cost.*

That is the answer — because it will not happen. Price above average cost *is*
supernormal profit, and in the long run entry has removed it. AR sits on AC.

Only 46% of students got that. And notice the trap: the option is completely true
for a monopoly. If you have merged the two structures in your head, it reads like
a correct statement and you move on.

One check kills it. **Can a new firm enter?** If yes, supernormal profit is
temporary. Always.

### [2:45] 常错考点 2 — excess capacity

Second one, and this is the one that decides the harder questions.

Because the demand curve slopes down, the long-run tangency is on the *falling*
part of the average cost curve — not at its minimum. The firm could produce more
and have lower average cost. It doesn't. That unused gap is excess capacity.

Perfect competition does not have this. Its demand curve is horizontal, so the
tangency lands exactly at the bottom of average cost. Minimum AC, no gap.

So: excess capacity is not a flaw in your diagram. It is the *result*, and it is
what separates monopolistic competition from perfect competition in the long run.

### [3:15] 原题 2 — the question from the start

Now back to where we started. October/November 2016, paper code 9708/32, question 13
— set again in February/March 2020, paper 9708/32, as question 11.

*Comparing the long-run outcome in monopolistic competition with perfect
competition, what would you most likely find?*

Four options. A: greater excess capacity in monopolistic competition. B: a higher
level of profit. C: a larger number of firms. D: a more price-elastic demand
curve.

**A is the answer.** Excess capacity — exactly what we just built.

**B was chosen by 46% of students in 2016, and 39% in 2020.** B says monopolistic
competition earns higher profit in the long run. But we established it: entry
competes profit away. Both structures end at normal profit. B is only true if you
think this is a monopoly.

That is the whole reason this question is hard. Not the economics. The
confusion.

### [4:00] Hook and CTA

Now — options C and D. Both wrong, both wrong for reasons that are *themselves*
examinable, and both catch large numbers of students every year.

C is about how many firms. D is about how elastic that demand curve really is,
and the answer is the opposite of what most people guess.

Those are 常错考点 3 and 4, and I'm out of time here — so they're in the next
video. Subscribe if you want it.

Or go faster: there's a free 8-question market-structures quiz linked below. Every
wrong option in it is a mistake examiners have actually written up. So when you
submit, you don't just get a score — you get told **which** misconception you
have, the full worked explanation for each one, and more practice on it.

That covers 考点 3 and 4 today, without waiting for me.

## Diagram brief

**One graphic, five stages.** The reveal *is* the teaching. Never show a finished
version first, and never cut away from it — the whole middle of the video lives
on this one asset.

**Layout.** Two panels, fixed side by side for the entire video, axes aligned at
the same scale. Left = MONOPOLY. Right = MONOPOLISTIC COMPETITION. They must
never move or resize, so every later change reads as a change in the *economics*,
not the camera.

**Stage 1 — "these look the same" (1:05).**
Build both panels simultaneously, same colours, same proportions, same shading:
downward-sloping AR, MR below it, MC and AC. Profit-maximising output at MR = MC.
Supernormal profit shaded in both. Draw them *deliberately similar*. The viewer
should think "those are the same graph." That reaction is the point of the video.

**Stage 2 — monopoly, time forward (1:20).**
Left panel only. Caption: *next year.* Nothing moves. Draw a wall at the panel
edge with entrant arrows bouncing off it. The profit block stays exactly where it
was. Hold two seconds longer than feels comfortable — the stillness is the idea.

**Stage 3 — monopolistic competition, time forward (1:28).**
Right panel only. Arrows enter from the side. AR slides left, MR with it, in three
discrete steps. The profit block shrinks each step. Settle at tangency: AR just
touching AC, profit block gone. This shrinking block is the single most important
motion in the video — give it the most visual weight.

**Stage 4 — 考点 1 callout (1:50).**
Right panel. Zoom on the tangency. Highlight that AR and AC meet at one point, and
label `P = AC → normal profit`. Dim everything else. Then briefly split-screen
the left panel's untouched profit block beside it, labelled `P > AC`. The contrast
is the mark.

**Stage 5 — 考点 2 callout (2:50).**
Right panel, zoom stays on AC. Drop a vertical from the tangency to the axis:
label `Q actual`. Drop a second vertical from the *minimum* of AC: label
`Q min AC`. Shade the gap between them and label it **`EXCESS CAPACITY`**.

Then, to the side, add a small third panel for perfect competition: horizontal AR
tangent to AC exactly at its minimum, so the two verticals coincide and the gap is
zero. This side-by-side is what makes the answer to 原题 2 obvious rather than
memorised.

**Requirements**

- The tangency must be visibly a *tangency*, not an intersection. Students cannot
  reproduce this under pressure, and it is the thing the diagram exists to teach.
- The excess-capacity gap must be large enough to read at phone size. If a true-
  to-scale drawing makes it tiny, exaggerate the curvature — say so on screen
  once (`not to scale`) and move on.
- Both panels stay on screen and aligned throughout. No panning.
- Build from scratch. Do not trace or reproduce any exam-board diagram, including
  the one in the Jun 2022 paper.
