## The one takeaway

> The difference is not in the diagram. It is in what happens *after* the
> diagram — and that is decided by barriers to entry.

## Script (432 words)

**[0:00]**
An examiner wrote this about a real exam essay: students confused monopolistic
competition with monopoly, and — their words — "very little, if anything, could
be rewarded."

Not a lost mark. The whole essay.

And that is AQA. Cambridge flags it. Edexcel flags it. OCR prints a box in their
report headed "Misconception" just to warn you off it. Four boards, same
confusion.

So let's kill it in two minutes.

**[0:12]**
First — why is this so easy to get wrong? Two reasons, and neither is your fault.

Reason one is the name. "Monopolistic" *contains* the word "monopoly". It sounds
like a type of monopoly. It isn't. It's closer to the opposite.

Reason two is the diagram. In the short run, these two models look almost
identical. Both firms face a downward-sloping demand curve. Both set price above
marginal cost. Both can make supernormal profit. If you're comparing the
short-run pictures, there is genuinely very little to separate them.

So stop comparing the short-run pictures. That is the trap.

**[0:40]**
Monopoly. One seller. That's the whole market. Barriers to entry are high —
maybe a patent, maybe a licence, maybe costs so large no one else can start.

Now here's the part that matters. That firm makes supernormal profit, and other
firms can *see* it. But they can't get in. The barrier holds. So next year the
profit is still there. And the year after.

**[1:15]**
Monopolistic competition. Many sellers, each one slightly different — different
brand, different location, different feel. That differentiation is why the
demand curve slopes down, same as monopoly. That's the resemblance.

Barriers to entry are low.

So run the same year forward. The firm makes supernormal profit. Other firms see
it. And this time — they walk in. New entrants take a share of demand. The
firm's demand curve shifts left, and keeps shifting, until supernormal profit is
gone.

Long run: normal profit only.

**[2:00]**
That is the whole distinction, and here's the test. One question:

*Can a new firm enter?*

If no — monopoly. Supernormal profit survives.
If yes — monopolistic competition. Supernormal profit is temporary.

Not the diagram. Not the number of sellers, really. Entry.

And notice what this gives you in an essay: the moment a question says "in the
long run", it is asking about entry. That's your signal.

**[2:30]**
I've built a short diagnostic on market structures. Every wrong answer in it is
a mistake examiners have actually written up — so it tells you *which* confusion
you have, not just a score. Link's below.

## Diagram brief

**One graphic, revealed in three stages.** The reveal *is* the teaching — do not
show the finished version first.

**Stage 1 — "these look the same" (0:25).**
Two panels side by side, labelled MONOPOLY and MONOPOLISTIC COMPETITION. Each
shows a downward-sloping AR, an MR below it, and an MC/ATC pair, with supernormal
profit shaded. Draw them *deliberately similar* — same shading colour, same
proportions. The viewer should think "those are the same graph." That reaction is
the point.

**Stage 2 — press play on monopoly (0:55).**
Left panel only. Nothing moves. Add a caption: *next year.* Still nothing moves.
The shaded profit block stays exactly where it is. Optionally draw a wall at the
edge of the panel with arrows bouncing off it — entrants trying and failing.

**Stage 3 — press play on monopolistic competition (1:35).**
Right panel only. Arrows enter from the side. The AR curve slides left, and MR
with it. The shaded profit block shrinks each step. Run three steps, then settle
at the tangency point — AR just touching ATC, profit block gone. Hold there.

**Requirements:**
- Both panels must stay visually aligned so Stage 2 and Stage 3 are comparable.
- The shrinking profit block in Stage 3 must be the eye-catching element. It is
  the single idea of the video.
- End state must clearly show *tangency*, not intersection. That tangency is
  what "normal profit in the long run" looks like, and it is the thing students
  cannot reproduce under pressure.
- No exam-board diagram may be traced or reproduced. Build from scratch.
